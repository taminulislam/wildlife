% Abstract and keywords

\abstract{Wildlife surveys ask how many animals are present, but the computer-vision
pipelines built to answer that question are optimized as detectors. (1) Background: we test
whether detection accuracy predicts counting accuracy on thermal road-transect video, and
localize where the two diverge. (2) Methods: we introduce a corpus of 32 fully annotated
FLIR thermal transects (521,930 frames, 236 individually tracked white-tailed deer, 21,646
boxes), benchmark twelve detectors on it, and build TRACT, a counting pipeline that
decomposes end-to-end error into three measurable stages --- whether an animal is detected at all, whether it
acquires its own candidate track, and whether it is finally counted. The confirmation rule
is fitted on 19 videos and frozen before evaluation on 13 the detector never trained on. (3) Results: the pipeline detects $98.8\pm5.3\%$ of held-out
animals, gives $88.0\pm8.7\%$ of them a distinct track, and counts $62.7\pm10.7\%$, at a mean
absolute error of $2.38\pm1.29$ animals per video (95\% intervals). Four independent interventions that each demonstrably improve candidate
generation make the final count worse, and learned confirmation heads all lose to a
three-parameter hand-tuned rule. (4) Conclusions: detection is not the
bottleneck in thermal wildlife counting. The confirmation stage is, and at this corpus size
it is limited by supervision rather than by model capacity.}

\keyword{thermal imaging; wildlife survey; animal counting; object detection; multi-object
tracking; white-tailed deer; benchmark dataset; error decomposition}
