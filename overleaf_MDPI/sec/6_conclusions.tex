\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to build a thermal wildlife counting system and found that the accuracy a
practitioner would try to buy with a better detector is being lost somewhere else. By
decomposing counting error into detection, association and confirmation, we showed that on
held-out video the pipeline finds 98.8\% of animals, gives 88.0\% a distinct track, and
counts 62.7\% --- and that four independent improvements to the first two stages each made
the third worse. Learned confirmers of three capacities lose to a three-parameter rule under
three evaluation protocols, and the rule's confidence condition turns out to be doing
duplicate suppression rather than the recall filtering it resembles: removing it recovers 23
points of coverage and costs two duplicates for every animal it returns.

The evidence points to supervision scarcity rather than model class as the reason
confirmation resists learning. The pools offer roughly two hundred positive tracks against
tens of thousands of negatives, and duplicate tracks sit \emph{between} the positives and
the false candidates in every feature we measured, so a confirmer receives contradictory
supervision on exactly the examples that distinguish counting from detection. The capacity
trend extrapolates to a crossover in the low thousands of positive tracks, an order of
magnitude beyond this corpus and a concrete target for the next dataset.

We release the dataset, per-individual annotations, evaluation harness and decomposition
tooling. The gap between 88\% reachable and 63\% counted is the most useful thing we can
hand to the next group working on this problem: it is large, precisely localized, and
entirely open.
