\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Wildlife Abundance and Density Estimation}
Every field method for estimating abundance carries assumptions whose violation biases the
result. Distance sampling fits a detection function to distances recorded along transects and
extrapolates to density; the \emph{Distance} software implements conventional,
multiple-covariate and mark--recapture engines, each relaxing progressively more assumptions,
but even the most flexible still requires near-certain detection on the line, no responsive
movement, and error-free distance measurement~\cite{thomas2010distance}. Buckland et
al.~\cite{buckland2023wildlife} argue that camera, acoustic and spatial capture--recapture
survey are displacing classical distance sampling faster than statistical ecology is adapting
to them. For unmarked populations, N-mixture models estimate abundance from spatially
replicated counts by treating site-level population size as a latent Poisson
variable~\cite{royle2004nmixture}; spatial capture--recapture extends this to camera-trap
encounters but requires individually identifiable animals~\cite{royle2009bayesian}, which
thermal imagery at our resolution cannot supply. Iijima~\cite{iijima2020review} surveys the
full landscape and concludes that no estimator is universally applicable. Recent work
addresses specific biases: movement compensation corrects the detection inflation caused by
animals encountering a moving observer~\cite{morgan2024wildlife}, a concern that applies
directly to road transects.

What none of this settles is whether an automated system can deliver a \emph{per-individual}
count --- one animal, one count --- rather than a density estimate conditioned on a detection
function. These methods model imperfect detection as a nuisance parameter; none attributes
the loss to a stage of an automated pipeline.

\subsection{Thermal and Infrared Imaging for Wildlife}
Thermal cameras detect animals by emitted heat, enabling nocturnal survey where visual methods
fail. Burke et al.~\cite{burke2019optimizing} identify the binding constraints: contrast loss
as ground temperature approaches body temperature, atmospheric absorption, vegetation
occlusion, and a requirement of roughly ten pixels on target for species identification --- and
their drone survey of riverine rabbits still needed ground confirmation of every sighting.
Karp~\cite{karp2020detecting} measured a 36\% detection probability for hare leverets over 109
handheld searches, with vegetation height dominating. Ul Haq et al.~\cite{ulhaq2021automated}
showed that transfer learning partially compensates for poor spatial resolution in airborne
thermal video, and Mowen et al.~\cite{mowen2022improving} classified animal poses from
vehicle-mounted FLIR imagery at 82\% accuracy while noting that a single-city dataset limits
generalisation. Reviews of remotely piloted survey~\cite{hyun2020remotely} and of monitoring
methods for large mammals~\cite{prosekov2020methods} both conclude that thermal sensors detect
homeothermic animals well but that automated species identification from thermal alone remains
unreliable. McMahon et al.~\cite{mcmahon2021comparing} compared UAS thermal survey against
pellet-group counts for white-tailed deer and obtained overlapping but very wide intervals,
4--24 deer\,km$^{-2}$. Nazir and Kaleem~\cite{nazir2021advances} note that thermal-domain work
lags the RGB domain in dataset scale and standardisation.

We found no thermal wildlife study that annotates individual animals as distinct tracks across
continuous video. Detection is evaluated per frame or per image, which leaves unanswered
whether the same animal is counted once or many times.

\subsection{Aerial and UAV Survey and Automated Counting}
Brown et al.~\cite{brown2022automated} tested detection across ground sample distances from
0.2 to 1.0\,m\,px$^{-1}$ and found that image detail, not network capacity, sets the limit.
Infantes et al.~\cite{infantes2022automated} built an end-to-end pinniped survey from flight
planning through orthomosaic to automatic body-size measurement.
Kline et al.~\cite{kline2024integrating} tracked zebra and giraffe from an autonomous UAV but
restricted analysis to herd centroids rather than individuals, and Mpouziotas et
al.~\cite{mpouziotas2023automated} reached 91.3\% AP on drone bird footage evaluated per frame
only. Kilfoil et al.~\cite{kilfoil2020using} found that both human and CNN counts of sea
cucumbers underestimate at high density. Tarling et al.~\cite{tarling2022deep} applied density
regression with uncertainty quantification to sonar imagery of fish schools, using
self-supervised pretraining to compensate for a small labelled set. Automated bird detection
has also been evaluated for airport strike management~\cite{oviedo2024automatic}.

Across this literature the dominant evaluation is per-image detection accuracy. Few studies
report tracking metrics, and we found none reporting distinct-individual count accuracy
against per-individual ground truth.

\subsection{Camera Traps, Domain Shift and Generalisation}
Deep learning for camera traps was catalysed by Norouzzadeh et
al.~\cite{norouzzadeh2018automatically}, who matched crowd-sourced labels on 3.2 million
Snapshot Serengeti images, and by Beery et al.~\cite{beery2018recognition}, who showed that
models trained at one camera location fail at unseen ones. Kellenberger et
al.~\cite{kellenberger2018detecting} found the same failure across terrain in aerial survey.
Singh et al.~\cite{singh2020animal} documented poor transfer from natural to man-made
environments, and Beery et al.~\cite{beery2020synthetic} showed synthetic renders reduce error
for rare classes only. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023lote} released a twelve-year longitudinal video
dataset and confirmed that web-image pretraining transfers poorly to camera-trap footage. Adam
et al.~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid10k} designed a similarity-aware split for a
re-identification benchmark specifically to prevent the visual-near-duplicate leakage that
inflates reported accuracy --- the same class of contamination our held-out protocol
(\cref{sec:heldout}) is built to exclude. Throughout this literature the unit of analysis is
the trigger event, and individual identity is assumed unknown unless the species carries
distinctive markings.

\subsection{Small-Object Detection}
COCO defines small as area below $32^2$~\cite{lin2014coco}, but wildlife at range falls well
under it. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2021nwd} show that IoU is catastrophically sensitive to
localisation error at small scale: a one-pixel shift on a $6\times6$ box drops IoU from 0.53 to
0.06, while the same shift on a $36\times36$ box drops it from 0.90 to 0.65. Their Normalized
Wasserstein Distance models boxes as Gaussians and gains 6.7 AP on tiny-object benchmarks. Zhu
et al.~\cite{zhu2021tph} added a transformer prediction head and attention to target the drone
scale distribution. This analysis bears directly on our result that IoU$\geq$0.50 re-ranks
detectors relative to a counting criterion (\cref{sec:choosing}): at 27 pixels the strict
threshold discards detections a counting task would accept.

\subsection{Detection Architectures}
Both one- and two-stage families appear in our benchmark: Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster} as
the two-stage baseline, ATSS~\cite{zhang2020atss} and TOOD~\cite{feng2021tood} as
anchor-assignment refinements, RTMDet~\cite{lyu2022rtmdet} as a modern convolutional one-stage
design, and the YOLO line~\cite{redmon2016yolo,wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10} as the practical
default. Query-based detectors --- DETR~\cite{carion2020detr} and DINO~\cite{zhang2023dino} ---
converge slowly and struggle on very small objects, which RT-DETR~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr} targets
directly. Recent reviews trace the YOLO line from v1 to v12~\cite{sapkota2025yoloreview} and
compare v8 through v11 architecturally, noting that some blocks are unchanged across versions
and that missing publications for several releases complicate
reproducibility~\cite{hidayatullah2025yolov8}. Our animals are a median $29\times24$ pixels,
squarely where these differences matter, and \cref{sec:detection} reports how each family
transfers under identical preprocessing and splits --- a comparison the literature rarely
offers, because each paper evaluates on its own data and augmentation pipeline.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking}
Tracking-by-detection dominates. SORT~\cite{bewley2016sort} and
DeepSORT~\cite{wojke2017deepsort} established the Kalman-plus-association template;
ByteTrack~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack} showed that associating low-confidence detections recovers
substantial recall; BoT-SORT~\cite{aharon2022botsort} added camera-motion compensation and an
appearance term. Our camera moves at 25--90\,km\,h$^{-1}$, so global motion compensation is not
optional; we use BoT-SORT with sparse optical-flow compensation and ablate its appearance term
in \cref{sec:inversion}. Evaluation has moved toward HOTA, which separates detection from
association accuracy, and Valmadre et al.~\cite{valmadre2021local} argue that global metrics
mask local failures. MOTRv2~\cite{zhang2023motrv2} shows that even end-to-end transformer
trackers benefit from strong detection priors. Our \reachedq/\primaryq/\countedq decomposition
parallels the detection/association split but adds a third axis --- confirmation --- that these
metrics do not measure. The distinction matters: MOT benchmarks score identity
\emph{preservation}, not whether an object should be counted at all.

\subsection{Counting as Its Own Task}
Density regression, pioneered by MCNN~\cite{zhang2016single}, estimates per-image counts
without detection, but the estimate is per frame and does not deduplicate across frames. That
suits dense scenes with hundreds of overlapping instances; ours is the opposite regime, sparse
instances each of which must be resolved as an individual because survey statistics are
computed per animal. Video-level counting is structurally different: it requires detection,
association, and a rule that decides whether an object is \emph{new}. Dolokov et
al.~\cite{dolokov2023upper} exploit a known maximum animal count to reduce identity switches in
laboratory mice, but report tracking quality rather than a unique-individual count. Our
contribution is to show that in the sparse regime the detection-then-count framing is not wrong
so much as \emph{mis-attributed}: the detector is near-saturated, and the accuracy a
practitioner would try to buy with a better backbone is lost downstream.

\subsection{Animal Re-Identification}
Individual identification from appearance is established for species with high-contrast
markings: photo-identification networks reach high accuracy on coat
patterns~\cite{miele2020revisiting}, deformable transformers reach 85\% mAP on cross-camera
tiger data~\cite{li2024redeformtr}, and WildlifeReID-10k spans 33 species and 10{,}000
identities~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid10k}. All operate on high-resolution crops of
distinctively patterned animals. \Cref{sec:reid} tests whether this transfers and finds that it
does not: at a median 27-pixel animal, appearance carries less identity signal than a
two-number size descriptor, and an ImageNet backbone scores \emph{below} plain geometry. We
therefore make no individual-identification claim and do not use appearance matching in the
reported system.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology and Calibration}
Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2023trustworthy} show that detection rankings fluctuate with the IoU
threshold and propose formal requirements that average precision does not satisfy. LRP
Error~\cite{oksuz2022lrp} separates localisation from classification quality and yields a
deployment threshold. Cai and Vasconcelos~\cite{cai2021cascade} document the high-quality
detection paradox, where raising the training IoU threshold causes overfitting as positives
vanish; Boundary IoU~\cite{cheng2021boundary} shows that mask IoU over-penalises small objects.
Our finding that IoU$\geq$0.50 re-ranks seven of eleven detectors by two or more places, and
that the best AP$_{50}$ model counts fewer animals than the one we adopt, instantiates exactly
the concern Nguyen et al.\ raise. On the calibration side, Rahman et
al.~\cite{rahman2021perframe} predict per-frame mAP from internal features to monitor
deployment without ground truth, and Tarling et al.~\cite{tarling2022deep} show that
uncertainty quantification improves both training and downstream biological decisions --- the
role our calibrated per-track posterior plays in \cref{sec:calibration}.

\subsection{White-Tailed Deer}
White-tailed deer are among the most intensively managed ungulates in North America.
Spotlight survey along road transects remains standard practice despite known road
bias~\cite{vercauteren2011managing}. Accurate density estimates matter beyond harvest
management: Bayesian weighted surveillance exploits heterogeneity in infection risk to detect
chronic wasting disease efficiently~\cite{jennelle2018applying}, and deer-to-human SARS-CoV-2
transmission was identified in animals sampled during routine CWD
surveillance~\cite{pickering2022divergent}. McMahon et al.~\cite{mcmahon2021comparing} remain
the closest published comparison for thermal deer survey, and report density rather than
individual counts. We found no published thermal or camera-based deer survey that reports a
per-individual count evaluated against per-individual ground truth over continuous video.

\Cref{tab:related} makes the gap specific rather than asserted. Its ``Individuals'' column
gives the number of distinct annotated animals and ``per-individual ID'' asks whether each is
annotated as its own track rather than as anonymous per-frame boxes; the last two columns are
the ones that isolate the gap, namely whether a count of distinct animals is reported end to
end and whether training and evaluation are separated by video or site rather than by frame. Thermal wildlife datasets with
per-individual track identity over continuous video are essentially absent: existing thermal
work annotates per-frame detections or static images, and the RGB video datasets that do carry
individual identity are either photo-identification collections of still
crops~\cite{adam2025wildlifereid10k} or controlled laboratory settings with a known, fixed
number of animals~\cite{dolokov2023upper} rather than open-field transects of unknown
population. Counting is nearly always evaluated as detection accuracy or per-image density
rather than as distinct-individual accuracy; we found no prior work reporting whether each real
animal was counted exactly once across a video. Train--test separation by video or site is
frequently unclear in the thermal and aerial literature, and our own measurement quantifies
what that costs --- evaluating on all 32 videos rather than the 13 held out inflates coverage
by 15.5 points, from 66.3\% to 81.8\% (\cref{tab:leak}). Most distinctively, no prior work
isolates \emph{which stage} of a detect--track--confirm pipeline loses animals. The literature
treats detection quality as the binding constraint; our decomposition shows it is not, and that
four independent improvements to candidate generation each make the final count worse. The
closest conceptual parallel is the detection/association split within HOTA, which measures
identity preservation but not whether an object should be counted at all --- a different
question when the output is a population tally rather than a surveillance feed.
