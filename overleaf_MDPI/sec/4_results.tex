F1 rises monotonically across \cref{tab:detectors} as the matching rule relaxes, and
centre-in-box tracks presence to within 0.032 for every model, so the criterion choice shifts
the level uniformly rather than favouring particular architectures.
\Cref{tab:detectors} shows first how little separates them: AP$_{50}$ spans 0.365--0.510,
with the top three within 0.004, across designs as different as Faster R-CNN, DINO and
YOLO12m. That narrow spread is the signature of a data-limited rather than an
architecture-limited regime, and \cref{fig:training} shows it is not an artefact of any
model being under-trained: within the budget each run either plateaus or passes its peak,
and the plateaus sit on top of one another. Two patterns in that figure matter for what
follows. Precision settles near 0.65--0.70 while recall settles near 0.40--0.45 in every
panel, which is the precision-heavy operating point \cref{sec:choosing} argues the counting
stage needs; and several models peak early and then decline, so the figures we report come
from the best checkpoint rather than the last. The five mmdetection models of
\cref{tab:detectors} are absent because their validation loop logs only the COCO evaluator's
output, which contains no scalar precision or recall, so they could contribute only a
different set of series. The one genuinely unsettled curve is DINO R50,
which is still oscillating between 0.15 and 0.30 mAP@50 at the point its budget expires, the slow convergence the query-based literature reports, visible directly.

The absolute values are low for two reasons, neither of them a model failure. The first is
geometric: for axis-aligned boxes of side $s$ offset by $d$ pixels,
$\mathrm{IoU}=(s-d)/(s+d)$, so at the corpus median $s=27$ a four-pixel centre error already
fails a 0.75 threshold and nine pixels fails 0.50. Scoring at strict IoU demands sub-pixel
localization on animals 27 pixels across with diffuse thermal boundaries. The second is that
these metrics count boxes rather than animals: an animal visible for 100 frames contributes
100 ground-truth boxes, and one found in half of them scores 0.5 recall even though counting
requires a single detection. The 11,476 background frames contribute nothing either way,
since precision, recall and F1 have no true-negative term. Counted per animal, the same
detectors that score around 0.50 box recall find 220 of the 235 annotated deer in at least
one frame, and the full pipeline reaches 82 of 83 on held-out video (\cref{sec:counting}).
The distance between 0.50 and 0.94 reflects two metrics answering different
questions, the first sign that detection accuracy does not predict counting ability.

The last column of \cref{tab:detectors} makes that gap explicit for every model. Measured
over 235 animals these are tighter than the counting figures: YOLO11m's 98.7\% carries a
95\% interval of $\pm2.4$ points, because the sample is the whole corpus rather than the
held-out subset. Measured per
animal rather than per box, recall runs from 0.821 to 0.996: \emph{every} architecture we
trained finds between 82\% and 99.6\% of the 235 individuals in at least one frame, including
models whose box recall sits near 0.45. This is the sharpest statement of the paper's premise: at the level a survey cares about, detection is very nearly solved \emph{on this corpus},
and the animals that go uncounted are lost after it. \Cref{sec:loso} tests how far that
survives a change of site and finds it weakens rather than holds, which is where the
qualification belongs. It also re-ranks the table a third time. RTMDet-m leads at
0.996 and every mmdetection model places above every YOLO variant, which is not an argument
for adopting one: their coverage is bought with false positives that the confirmation stage
cannot absorb, as \cref{sec:inversion} shows and \cref{sec:choosing} anticipates. What the
column establishes is that the choice between these detectors is not a choice about which
animals can be found.

A third pattern belongs to individual rows rather than to the table as a whole, and it is
what makes an entry like YOLO12m's 0.929 precision against 0.452 recall look
self-contradictory. It is not. The two numbers describe one conservative operating point:
of YOLO12m's errors at confidence 0.25, 1530 are missed boxes and 96 are false ones, so
\textbf{94\% of its error is silence rather than mistake}. A model that is quiet is not a
model that is wrong, and the distinction matters downstream, because the two failures cost
the confirmation stage very different amounts.

\Cref{tab:threshold} gives the sensitivity. At 0.10 the same YOLO12m weights score 0.711
precision and 0.653 recall, so the low recall in \cref{tab:detectors} is a property of the
reporting threshold rather than of the model. This also means the figure in
\cref{tab:detectors} is not the operating point of the system this paper describes:
\textbf{the counting pipeline runs the detector at confidence 0.10}, where recall is 20
points higher, and 0.25 is reported only because it is the conventional threshold for a
detection table. Recall is further understated by the matching rule: relaxing IoU$\geq$0.50 to presence cuts YOLO12m's false positives from 307 to 96 while its misses fall only from
1741 to 1530, because a looser criterion can credit a box that was placed a few pixels off
an animal but cannot recover an animal the detector never fired on.

The ordering of \cref{tab:threshold} is for reading rather than a verdict, because F1 weights
precision and recall equally, the balance \cref{sec:choosing} argues is ill-suited to a counting pipeline. Under the precision-weighted $F_{0.5}$ we actually select on, YOLO11m leads at 0.775
against YOLO12m's 0.767. What matters more than the size of the trade is its direction, which
is not symmetric: a detector already operating at high recall has nowhere left to go. Dropping
RT-DETR-L to 0.10 buys 231 additional true boxes and costs 7433 false ones, reducing precision to 0.187, not a candidate pool any confirmation stage can work with, which is the
argument of \cref{sec:inversion} arriving one stage earlier.

One architectural pattern survives every criterion. The query-based detectors underperform:
DINO R50 reaches 0.365 AP$_{50}$ on a full native schedule, below plain Faster R-CNN, and
Deformable DETR's training exhausted GPU memory at epoch 5 and was not rerun at a smaller batch. RT-DETR-L, which targets exactly this, recovers to 0.434 but still trails the convolutional one-stage models.

\subsection{Choosing a Detector for Counting}
\label{sec:choosing}

The narrow spread in aggregate scores conceals a substantial difference in operating point.
YOLO12m and YOLOv9m sit at 0.929 and 0.917 precision against roughly 0.45 recall, while
RTMDet-m and Faster R-CNN R50 occupy the opposite end, reaching 0.779 and 0.723 recall at
0.33 and 0.39 precision. Since a matching criterion determines how errors of each type are
counted, rank depends on which criterion is applied. Comparing the two orderings in
\cref{tab:detectors}, seven of eleven models move at least two places between them: YOLOv10m
falls from 2nd to 8th and ATSS R50 climbs from 8th to 2nd, a six-place exchange between a
precision-tuned and a recall-tuned model. Selecting a detector on its detection metric does
not select the model that counts best.

Both AP$_{50}$ and F1 weight precision and recall equally, which does not reflect the cost
structure of the stage that consumes these detections. The confirmation step must reject
tens of thousands of false candidates against roughly two hundred true ones
(\cref{sec:counting}), so a false positive and a false negative are not equally expensive to
it, and \cref{sec:inversion} shows that enlarging the candidate pool lowers the final count rather than raising it. We therefore summarize with the precision-weighted
$F_{0.5}$ in the last column of \cref{tab:detectors}, fixing $\beta$ at its conventional
value rather than selecting it, so $F_{0.5}=1.25\,PR/(0.25P+R)$. YOLO11m ranks first at
0.775, ahead of YOLO12m at 0.767 and YOLOv9m at 0.759. That margin of 0.008 makes the top three effectively equivalent; the
substantive separation is from the recall-oriented models at 0.368--0.433. We adopt
\textbf{YOLO11m at 640\,px} as the production detector, and every counting result in this
paper uses it.

Resolution is the one lever that behaves unpredictably. \Cref{tab:resolution} shows 1280\,px raising YOLOv9m on every metric and lowering YOLOv10m on every one; at 640 the two
are within 0.004 AP$_{50}$, at 1280 they diverge by 0.047. AP-small falls for both, so the
extra resolution buys overall precision and recall rather than the smallest animals.

That gain does not survive the pipeline. YOLOv9m at 1280\,px is the strongest detector in
this section by AP$_{50}$ (0.516 against YOLO11m's 0.460), so we ran it through the complete
counting pipeline under the protocol of \cref{sec:heldout}. It counts \emph{fewer} animals:
MAE 2.54 and 54 of 83 held-out animals, against 2.38 and 55 of 83 for YOLO11m at 640\,px.
A 12\% advantage in detection accuracy becomes a small deficit in counting accuracy, which
is why the production detector is not the one this section ranks highest, and the first
direct evidence for the divergence the rest of the paper characterizes.

upstream. NShelbyRd is the largest single loss in the corpus, shedding 5 animals to detection

Two videos return a count of zero, and they are worth naming rather than leaving for the
reader to find. AquaCultureRd holds 3 animals and reaches 1; TouchofNature holds 2 and
reaches 1. Both are \emph{detection} failures: the animals are at the sensor's limit, near

error of 3 and 2 animals understates how these rows read: on a transect holding three animals,
missing all three leaves the count at zero, and the mean absolute error of 2.38 animals per video
in \cref{tab:counting} conceals that a small-count transect can be off by 100\% while
contributing little to the mean. This is the strongest argument in the paper for reporting the
decomposition per video rather than only in aggregate.

Under the stricter identity-matched criterion of \cref{sec:decomposition}, animals
covered by an \emph{accepted} track, rather than per-video count agreement, and by it the figure is 47/83 = 56.6\%. The 10-point gap is coincidental agreement: accepted tracks that do not land
on an animal offsetting animals that were missed. The capped aggregate is our headline
figure, for comparability with the counting literature; the identity-matched figure is
reported alongside it because the two answer different questions.

\subsection{Improving Candidate Generation Makes Counting Worse}
\label{sec:inversion}

This is the paper's central experimental result. We improved the candidate pool four
independent ways and measured the effect on the decomposition and on the final count.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/fig2_inversion.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The inversion.} As candidate generation improves, more animals become
  reachable and fewer are counted. Both series are animals out of 83 and share one
  axis.\label{fig:inversion}}
\end{figure}

\Cref{tab:ablationmain} and \cref{fig:inversion} give the result, one row per
intervention; the four candidate pools are labelled C, E, F and G there and referred to by
those letters throughout. Each
does what it was designed to do:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,labelsep=4.5mm]
\item \textbf{Lowering detector confidence} 0.10$\to$0.02 and \textbf{removing the tracker's
track-ini\-tial\-i\-za\-tion gate} raised \reachedq from 74 to 79. Lowering confidence \emph{alone}
recovered zero additional animals: the gate, not the threshold, was the binding constraint: BoT-SORT will extend a track from a low-confidence detection but will not \emph{start}
one, so faint animals were invisible to counting regardless of detector threshold.
\item \textbf{Loosening NMS} to IoU 0.90 addressed animals standing close enough that one
box suppressed another; it added 4 \primaryq at the cost of $5\times$ the candidates.
\item \textbf{Appearance re-identification} produced the best pool by every measure: 82 of
83 animals reached, 73 with their own track, at 27,679 candidates, fewer than a third of
pool F's for four more \primaryq. It genuinely works: it merges fragments of one animal
rather than merely adding candidates.
\end{itemize}

And the count falls, monotonically in candidate volume: 55, 54, 52, 41.

One qualification belongs here before the interpretation. With 83 animals, a difference of two
or three is not resolvable: the 95\% interval on pool C's 55 is $\pm$9 animals, and pools E and
G lie inside it. No single pairwise comparison in this table is individually significant, and
we do not claim one. The evidence is the \emph{direction}, which is the same for four independent interventions (a result with probability $2^{-4}$ under the null that pool improvements are neutral), together with pool F, whose 41 of 83 does fall outside pool C's
interval. A reader should take from this that improving the pool never helped, not that any
particular pool is significantly worse than another. The confirmation
stage cannot exploit a pool it did not shrink. Each intervention adds far more negatives than positives: pool G has 219 positive tracks against 27,460 negatives, and a
three-parameter rule tuned to survive that ratio must be aggressive enough to reject real
animals along with the noise.

threshold and pointed us at the tracker's initialization gate.

\Cref{fig:qualitative} shows what the association stage does on held-out video under the two
conditions that make counting fail. The first is overlap. When two animals stand close
enough that their boxes intersect, a tracker that leans on spatial proximity will hand both
to one identity, and the pair is counted once; the top row and the bottom-right frame each
contain such a pair, and in both the animals are carried as separate tracks with separate
identities. The top row is the same scene 0.3\,s apart, with five animals tracked and five

the camera translates beneath them. The second is low contrast. In the bottom-right frame two animals of 42 and 50\,px sit
against an almost uniform thermal field, overlapping in image space and individually close to indistinguishable, near the point where a human reader would hesitate to call them animals at all. They are still carried as two tracks. Overlap and low contrast are the two

does so here. Persistence
is visible directly in the top row: the two frames are 0.3\,s apart, the whole scene
translates between them as the vehicle moves, and the identities survive the translation
because motion compensation removes the camera's contribution before association. The
bottom-left frame shows the range spread a single transect frame can contain (87\,px and 22\,px animals simultaneously), with the calibrated score falling as the animal recedes,
which is the behaviour a per-track posterior is meant to have.

\subsection{What the Confidence Condition Buys}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The confirmation rule's three parameters are hand-set, and the confidence condition
$c\ge0.65$ is the one a reader is most likely to challenge. The detector did find those
animals; discarding a candidate because its five best frames average below 0.65 looks like
throwing away recall that was already paid for. This subsection measures exactly that, and
the answer is more interesting than a threshold defence: the condition is not a recall
filter at all.

\Cref{tab:confsens} holds the other two parameters at their published values and moves only
$c$. Three things follow.

First, \textbf{the objection is half right}. Removing the condition entirely raises held-out
coverage from 66.3\% to 89.2\%. That is the largest coverage gain produced by any single
change in this paper, larger than all four candidate-generation interventions of
\cref{sec:inversion}, none of which raised coverage at all. Those animals really were being
discarded, and they really do come back.

Second, \textbf{they do not come back alone}. The pipeline predicts 111 animals where 83
exist. Over-counts rise from 3 to 37 while under-counts fall only from 28 to 9, so MAE rises from 2.38 to 3.54 and the bias flips from $-1.92$ to $+2.15$. In animals: 53
additional tracks are accepted, of which 19 are animals that were previously missed and 34
are not, so the pipeline buys each recovered animal at the price of roughly two duplicates.

Third, and this is the substantive point, \textbf{the condition is a duplicate suppressor
rather than a detector threshold}. A fragment sitting on an animal that has already been
counted is a true detection, since an animal really is there, so nothing in the question
``is this a deer?'' separates it from a first sighting. Sustained high confidence across a
track is the cheapest signal available that does separate them, because a duplicate fragment is typically short and low-scoring where the animal's primary track is neither. That is
why $c$ cannot be relaxed to recover recall: recall and duplicate suppression are being
bought with the same parameter.

A practical corollary is visible in the bias column. The MAE-optimal setting is deliberately
conservative, under-counting by 1.9 animals per video. A survey that needs an approximately
\emph{unbiased} estimator rather than a minimum-error one should set $c\approx0.45$, where
the bias is $-0.15$, and accept 1.0 more MAE for it. We report the MAE-optimal rule as the
headline because it is the conventional choice, but which of the two a practitioner wants
depends on whether their downstream statistic is sensitive to variance or to bias.

One objection remains: that \cref{tab:confsens} moves a single parameter and therefore
overstates its importance. We repeated the sweep with $m$ and $s$ re-fitted on the 19
detector-training videos for \emph{each} value of $c$, so the confidence condition is never
blamed for a companion parameter left at a stale value. The conclusion strengthens rather
than softens. Given the freedom to compensate, the grid search responds to a lowered $c$ by
tightening the span constraint from 0 to 1\,s, buying precision with duration instead, and the substitution does not pay: at $c=0$ the held-out MAE is 3.69 against 2.38, and
coverage falls to 60.2\%, \emph{below} the frozen variant, because a one-second minimum
discards short tracks belonging to real animals. Across the full $7\times6\times9=378$-cell
grid only two values of $c$, 0.55 and 0.65, appear among the twelve best cells by fit MAE.

That sweep also exposes a redundancy we report rather than hide. The three best cells in the
grid are the same $c=0.65$ rule with the span parameter at 0, 0.1 and 0.25, all giving
identical fits: $s$ is \emph{inert} at the operating point, so the published rule is
effectively two parameters wearing a third. We keep the third because it is what the sweep
uses to compensate when $c$ is lowered, which is the behaviour that makes the re-fitted
comparison informative.

\subsection{The Rule Is Not the Bottleneck}
\label{sec:learned}

\Cref{sec:sensitivity} shows the rule's parameters are load-bearing rather than arbitrary.
The remaining question is whether the \emph{form} of the rule is what limits the count. If
so, replacing it with a learned confirmer should help. We tested that under three protocols
and it does not.

Because a negative result about learning is only as strong as the learners behind it, we
state them in full. Every confirmer consumes the same input: one candidate track as a
variable-length sequence of ten per-detection features: confidence, box centre, width, height, $\sqrt{\text{area}}$, the frame-to-frame deltas of centre and $\sqrt{\text{area}}$,
and the time gap to the previous detection, with positions and sizes normalised by frame
size, so that flicker appears as a large time gap and ego-motion as a systematic centre
drift. Eight further features describe the track's \emph{competing group}, the tracks
overlapping it in time whose mean boxes intersect it: group size, the track's length as a
fraction of the longest in its group, and the group's confidence and duration statistics.
These are the features a duplicate must be separated on, since a duplicate differs from its
primary not in appearance but in being the shorter, weaker member of a group. Appearance is
deliberately excluded, both because \cref{sec:reid} shows it carries little signal at this
pixel scale and because embeddings would let a 60k-parameter model memorise 32 videos.

The learners span three orders of magnitude in capacity: logistic regression on pooled
summary statistics (13 parameters); gradient boosting, both depth-3 at 200 iterations
($\sim$$10^3$) and depth-1 stumps ($\sim$40 effective parameters); and a temporal transformer
(2 pre-norm encoder layers, $d_{\text{model}}=64$, 4 heads, dropout 0.2, sinusoidal position
encoding, masked attention pooling so a 3-detection track and a 300-detection track pool
comparably, cross-track context fused after pooling; $6\times10^4$ parameters). Class
imbalance, 207 primary tracks against 6801 duplicates and false candidates in pool C, is
handled by inverse-frequency positive weighting in the loss
($w_+=N_-/N_+\approx33$); splits are by video throughout, reusing the detector's assignment so
no video appears in two splits. We did \emph{not} attempt hard-negative mining or
cost-sensitive losses beyond that weighting. We report this as a limitation rather than a
defence: it is possible that a better-tuned learner closes the gap, but the monotone capacity
trend below is evidence about sample size rather than about tuning, and a tuning fix would
have to explain why the \emph{smallest} models do best.

\Cref{tab:confirmers} gives the held-out comparison. Every learned method loses on pool C: gradient boosting and the temporal transformer both at 2.85 MAE and logistic regression at 3.00, against the rule's 2.38. On the richer pool G the transformer only ties, at 2.77. Every learned variant \emph{under-counts harder} than the rule, indicating that they
reject additional candidates rather than recovering the animals the rule discards.

Two further protocols agree. Under 8-fold cross-validation over videos the rule again wins
(1.88 MAE), and the pattern across that sweep is diagnostic: performance declines monotonically with model capacity: logistic regression at 13 parameters reaches 3.41,
gradient boosting at $10^3$ reaches 2.41--2.66, and the transformer at $6\times10^4$ reaches
2.81, while shrinking a gradient-boosted model to depth-1 stumps ($\sim$40 effective
parameters) recovers nearly the whole gap and achieves the best RMSE of any method. This is
a variance limit, not an architectural one.

We also trained directly on the counting objective, letting fragments share probability mass
so the loss is $|\sum_i p_i - c|$ rather than per-track cross-entropy. It ranked last of the variants tested, which rules out ``the rule wins because it optimizes MAE directly'' as the
explanation.

Having established \emph{where} the animals are lost, we ask \emph{which} animals and why.

\subsection{Duplicate Suppression at the Frame and Track Level}
\label{sec:nms}

Since the negatives that limit counting are duplicates, the natural question is whether
better duplicate suppression solves it. We tested the two levels at which suppression can
act, and neither reaches the duplicates that matter.

At the \emph{frame} level, non-maximum suppression is already the mechanism, and
\cref{tab:ablationmain} contains the sweep: raising the NMS IoU threshold from 0.50 to 0.90 (suppressing less, so that more overlapping boxes survive) multiplies the candidate pool
thirteenfold and produces the worst count in the paper, 41 of 83. Frame-level suppression is
therefore load-bearing in the direction of \emph{more} suppression, not less. We did not test
Soft-NMS~\cite{bodla2017softnms} or Confluence~\cite{shuai2021confluence}, and the reason is
structural rather than incidental: both decide between boxes present in the same frame, while
the duplicates that cost counts here are separate tracks on the same animal, often seconds
apart, that no frame-level rule can see. A frame-level method could at best change how
candidates are formed, and \cref{sec:inversion} shows that every change to candidate formation
we made left the count unchanged or lower.

At the \emph{track} level we did test clustering, which is the operation matching the problem:
accept tracks that lie on an animal, then merge the accepted tracks into animals. Stage one becomes an easier learning problem: ``is this track on a deer at all'' has 632 unambiguous
positives instead of 207 contested ones, and stage two is geometry: tracks overlapping in
time within a box-scale of each other are one animal, and the count is the number of clusters.
It reaches MAE 3.66 against the rule's 1.88 on the same 32-video protocol, and the error
changes character rather than shrinking: bias falls to $-0.34$ from $-0.81$, but over-counts
rise to 53 and under-counts to 64, against 17 and 43. The clustering is nearly unbiased and
much noisier, which is what happens when merging decisions are made on geometry alone at 27
pixels. This is the same trade as \cref{tab:confsens}: the methods that recover animals admit
duplicates at a similar rate, and the published rule sits where the two costs balance.

from a site the detector has seen. \Cref{tab:loso} asks the stricter question: train on three

detector that never saw its site. The confirmation rule is \emph{not} refitted per fold: the published $(m,s,c)$ is applied unchanged, which is the honest reading of ``does this
pipeline transfer''.

Two things follow, and the second is the more useful. First, detection transfers well but not
perfectly: \reachedq falls from 98.8\% within-site to $90.7 \pm 3.7\%$ across sites and
\primaryq to $78.0 \pm 5.2\%$. These are measured over all 236 animals rather than the 83 of
the main protocol, so they are the tighter estimates in the paper. The claim that detection is
near-saturated should therefore be read as within-corpus; across sites a real detection gap
opens, and \cref{sec:discussion} treats that as the boundary of the claim rather than a
detail.

Second, \countedq falls further and unevenly, 51.7\% pooled, but from 69\% of SHB's animals
down to none of MAS's, and the ``Max $c$'' column names the cause. The confirmation rule's
$c\ge0.65$ is an \emph{absolute} threshold on a detector score, and a detector trained without
a site scores that site systematically lower: on the MAS fold no track anywhere reaches 0.585,
so a threshold at 0.65 accepts nothing and the pipeline returns zero on 15 animals it detected
12 of. This is not a new failure mode but the one \cref{sec:sensitivity} already identified,
arriving through a different door: the confidence condition is doing duplicate suppression on
a score scale that is itself site-dependent.

The obvious repair does not work, which is informative. Replacing the absolute threshold with a fold-relative one, accepting the same \emph{fraction} of long tracks that $c\ge0.65$ accepts on pool C, restores coverage to $80.9 \pm 5.0\%$ and destroys the count, at MAE 8.84 and a
bias of $+6.03$ animals per video against $-2.94$ for the frozen rule. The cross-site folds
generate more long tracks than the within-site pool, so a fixed acceptance fraction admits
duplicates faster than it recovers animals. That is the inversion of \cref{sec:inversion}
reappearing across sites, and it says that transferring this pipeline to a new site requires
re-fitting the threshold on labelled data from that site, not a scale-free reformulation of
it. Quantifying how much labelled data that takes is the most practically valuable open
question this corpus leaves.

\subsection{Which Animals Are Missed}
\label{sec:analysis}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/fig4_scale.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{A hole in the training distribution.} The training split contains no box
  above 96\,px. Three missed animals sit at 104, 149 and 157\,px, one of them never
  detected at all. A detector that finds 27\,px animals does not miss a 149\,px one unless it
  has never seen one.\label{fig:scale}}
\end{figure}

Of the 83 held-out animals, the detector never fires on 9 (median 20\,px, at the sensor's
limit) and the rule rejects 27 that it did find. Of those 27, \textbf{26 fail the confidence
condition} and one fails purely on track length: their five best detections average below
0.65, though five do reach 0.65 in a single frame, so the failure is one of sustained rather
than peak confidence. Those 26 are the entire remaining opportunity, though \cref{sec:sensitivity} shows they cannot be recovered by moving the threshold that
rejects them, since doing so admits roughly two duplicates for each. We verified by direct
inspection that none of the 36 are annotation errors: each was rendered with its ground-truth box at three points in its track, and all are real animals.

The three groups are separated by degree rather than by kind, which is the reason
\cref{sec:learned} finds what it finds. Counted animals have a median box of 38.1\,px, 102
annotated frames and candidate confidence 0.76; the rejected group sits at 28.0\,px, 47
frames and 0.52; the undetected at 19.9\,px and 47 frames. The rejected animals lie between
the other two on every measure, so no threshold on any one of them separates them cleanly.

That inspection produced an unexpected finding. Three of the missed animals are not small but
very \emph{large}: 104, 149 and 157\,px against a corpus median of 27, and the 149\,px animal
was never detected at all. \Cref{fig:scale} gives the likely explanation: the largest
training box is 95.6\,px and the split contains \emph{zero} boxes at or above 100\,px, so all
three sit outside the annotated size distribution. Scale augmentation does present enlarged
copies, so the detector is not strictly unexposed to large objects, but under the symmetric
sampling used here an enlargement beyond $1.5\times$ is never drawn. The mirror image of the
small-object problem is visible here: detector performance tracks example density, and is lowest where examples are scarcest.

The remedy is straightforward to state, and we do not evaluate it here. Covering the upper
tail requires sampling the resize factor from an explicit asymmetric range rather than
symmetrically about unity, so that a 96\,px box can be presented at twice its size without
the same draw shrinking a 27\,px animal to sub-pixel; Ultralytics supports such a range from
version 8.4. Whether it recovers these animals is left to future work. The loss is
concentrated in any case: the one video containing them counts 3 of 8, three of its five
misses being the oversized individuals, so this is worth roughly 4 points of the headline
figure.

\subsection{Appearance Re-Identification Does Not Work at 27 Pixels}
\label{sec:reid}

Appearance-based individual re-identification is the natural proposal for the association
stage, and it is the one component we tested and \emph{rejected on the evidence}. This
subsection records why, because the negative result is more useful than the proposal: at this
pixel scale appearance carries less identity information than a two-number bounding box, and
a system built on it would be reporting range, not identity. Because the annotation gives one
track per individual, the corpus already contains identity labels and the question can be
settled empirically rather than argued. We evaluate closed-set
re-identification on \textbf{69 animals in 7 videos the detector never trained on}: each
track is split temporally, the first half forms the gallery and the second half the query.
Within video is the \emph{ceiling}, since both halves share range, pose and thermal
calibration, so a low score is decisive.

A trained thermal encoder reaches rank-1 $0.899 \pm 0.093$ (\cref{tab:reid}), which sounds
like working re-identification until the decomposition. \textbf{Box size alone reaches
$0.638 \pm 0.118$}, and within
a video an animal's range changes slowly, so most of what looks like identification is
matching \emph{distance}. Appearance in isolation carries little signal: mean intensity carries exactly
chance information, silhouette with scale removed reaches 0.435, and a pretrained CNN lands
below plain geometry. At a median $29\times24$ pixels there is not enough detail to key an
identity to, so we describe the system as performing \emph{track association}, preventing
double counting within a survey, and make no individual-identification claim. Appearance
does help the \emph{association} stage, which is a far easier problem: enabling it gives the
best candidate pool in the paper, 82 of 83 animals reached and 73 given their own track
(\cref{tab:ablationmain}). That pool still counts three fewer animals than the baseline,
so better association does not translate into a better count, the same pattern every other
pool improvement follows. The conclusion for practice is therefore double, and both halves
are negative: appearance cannot identify individual deer at this resolution, and even where
it demonstrably improves association it does not improve the count. \textbf{We do not use
appearance re-identification in the reported system}, and we would advise against building a
thermal survey pipeline on it until the imagery is several times larger than 27 pixels.
