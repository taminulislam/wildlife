\section{Detection Benchmark}
\label{sec:detection}

We first establish that the detection stage is not the limiting factor. Twelve
architectures spanning two-stage, one-stage and query-based designs were trained on the
identical site-stratified split with identical preprocessing, which bounds how much of the
counting error any choice of backbone could account for.

\Cref{tab:detectors} reports every model on identical data under both a standard metric and
the presence criterion this task requires. Four observations shape the rest of the paper.

\paragraph{These numbers count boxes, not animals, and the distinction is large.}
Every frame of every animal is scored separately, so an animal visible for 100 frames
contributes 100 ground-truth boxes and one found in half of them scores 0.5 recall --- even
though a single detection is all that counting requires. The 11,476 background frames
contribute nothing at all, because precision, recall and F1 have no true-negative term, so a
detector that correctly fires on none of them earns no credit for it. Read per animal rather
than per box, the same detectors that score $\approx$0.50 box recall in \cref{tab:detectors}
find 220 of the 235 individually annotated deer in at least one frame, and the full pipeline
of \cref{sec:counting} reaches 82 of 83 on held-out video. The gap between 0.50 and 0.94 is
not a discrepancy: it is two metrics answering different questions, and it is the first
indication that detection accuracy is a poor proxy for counting ability.

\paragraph{The spread across architectures is narrow.} AP$_{50}$ ranges 0.365--0.510 across
twelve architectures spanning two-stage, one-stage and query-based designs, with the top
three within 0.004 of each other. That is the signature of a data-limited rather than an
architecture-limited regime, and it bounds how much of the counting error in
\cref{sec:counting} any choice of backbone could account for.

\paragraph{Strict-IoU metrics compress at this object size, for geometric reasons.} For two
axis-aligned boxes of side $s$ whose centres differ by $d$ pixels,
$\mathrm{IoU}=(s-d)/(s+d)$. At the corpus median $s=27$, a \emph{four-pixel} centre error
already drops IoU to $0.742$ and fails a 0.75 threshold; nine pixels fails 0.50. Sub-pixel
localization is therefore required to score at the upper thresholds, on animals whose entire
extent is 27 pixels and whose thermal boundary is diffuse. Size-stratified COCO metrics inherit that
ceiling: on the 71\% of the corpus below $32^2$, YOLO11m scores 0.081 against 0.291 on
medium objects, a factor of 3.6, and the gap is consistent across both families. This is
the first quantitative argument for the criterion of \cref{sec:criterion}.

\paragraph{The models trade precision against recall, and the trade decides the ranking.}
YOLO12m and YOLOv9m operate at 0.929 and 0.917 precision against roughly 0.45 recall;
RTMDet-m and Faster R-CNN R50 invert it, at 0.779 and 0.723 recall against 0.33 and 0.39
precision. Because they sit on opposite sides of that trade, AP$_{50}$ rank and F1 rank
diverge: RTMDet-m is 4th on AP$_{50}$ and last on F1, while YOLOv8m is 8th on AP$_{50}$ and
1st on F1. Which operating point is preferable is not a detection question --- it depends on
whether the downstream stage can filter false candidates, which \cref{sec:counting}
addresses directly.

\paragraph{Query-based detectors underperform.} DINO R50 reaches 0.365 AP$_{50}$ on a full
native schedule, below plain Faster R-CNN, and Deformable DETR did not converge within
budget. This is consistent with the known difficulty query-based detectors have with very
small objects, and it holds for both query-based models tested. RT-DETR-L, which targets
that weakness, recovers to 0.434 but still trails the convolutional one-stage models.

\subsection{The consequence for detector selection}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_criterion.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Rank under IoU$\geq$0.50 does not predict rank under the counting
  criterion.} Eight of eleven models shift by two or more places; YOLOv10m falls from 2nd to
  9th. Choosing a detector for a counting system by its detection metric selects the wrong
  model.}
  \label{fig:criterion}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:criterion} plots the same data as a rank change. Eight of the eleven models move
at least two places and YOLOv10m falls seven, from 2nd under IoU$\geq$0.50 to 9th under the
counting criterion. Selecting a detector for a counting pipeline on its detection metric
therefore does not select the model that counts best.

We adopt YOLO11m as the production detector for every counting experiment that follows. It
is not the single best model under the counting criterion --- the top five are separated by
0.015 F1, which is inside the noise of a 32-video corpus --- but it has the highest precision
of the roster (0.732), and \cref{sec:counting} shows that precision, not recall, is what the
downstream confirmation stage is starved of.

\Cref{tab:resolution} shows that ``higher resolution helps small objects'' does not hold as
a general claim on this data. It helps one architecture and hurts another, and AP-small
declines for both even where overall mAP50 rises. The best detector at 1280\,px (YOLOv9m) is also
not the best counter (\cref{sec:counting}), an early instance of the divergence this paper
characterizes.
