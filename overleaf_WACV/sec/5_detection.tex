\section{Detection Benchmark}
\label{sec:detection}

We first establish that the detection stage is not the limiting factor, by benchmarking it
thoroughly enough that a reviewer need not wonder whether a better backbone would close the
gap. Thirteen architectures were trained on the identical site-stratified split with
identical preprocessing.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

All models consume CLAHE-normalized~\cite{zuiderveld1994clahe} frames, applied identically
at training and inference. Thermal transects have low and drifting global contrast; local
histogram equalization is worth more than any architectural choice we tested
(\cref{tab:ablations}, test mAP50 $0.519$ with, $0.299$ without).

\Cref{tab:detectors} gives the standard metric. Four observations shape the rest of the
paper.

\paragraph{The spread is narrow.} 0.365--0.498 across thirteen architectures spanning
two-stage, one-stage, and query-based designs. No architecture is dramatically better,
which is the signature of a data-limited rather than architecture-limited regime.

\paragraph{Query-based detectors underperform.} DINO R50 reaches 0.365 on a full native
schedule --- below plain Faster R-CNN --- and Deformable DETR did not converge within our
compute budget. This is consistent with the known weakness of query-based detectors on very
small objects and is worth stating explicitly for practitioners choosing a backbone for
thermal wildlife work.

\paragraph{False positives are near zero} (0.000--0.005 per background frame for the YOLO
family; RT-DETR-L at 0.044 is the outlier). The regime is recall-limited, not
precision-limited, which is what later justifies running the detector at very low confidence
and letting a downstream stage filter --- and what makes the results of \cref{sec:counting}
surprising.

\paragraph{Localization, not detection, is the weak axis.} AP@0.50 is 0.459 against AP@0.75
of 0.097 for the same model. The models find the animals; they place the boxes loosely. That
gap is the first sign that a strict-IoU metric is answering the wrong question here, which
\cref{sec:criterion} takes up.

\subsection{The criterion re-ranks the models}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_criterion.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Rank under IoU$\geq$0.50 does not predict rank under the counting
  criterion.} Five of twelve models shift by two or more places; YOLOv10m falls from 3rd to
  8th. Choosing a detector for a counting system by its detection metric selects the wrong
  model.}
  \label{fig:criterion}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:criterion} compares each model's F1 under IoU$\geq$0.50 against the any-overlap
criterion on the held-out test split. The top two are stable, but the middle of the
field reshuffles: eight of twelve models move at least two places and YOLOv10m falls seven.
The practical implication is direct --- a practitioner selecting a detector for a counting
pipeline using the standard metric will not select the model that counts best.

We adopt YOLO11m as the production detector for every counting experiment that follows. It
is not the single best model under the counting criterion --- the top five are separated by
0.015 F1, which is inside the noise of a 32-video corpus --- but it has the highest precision
of the roster (0.732), and \cref{sec:counting} shows that precision, not recall, is what the
downstream confirmation stage is starved of.

\caption{\textbf{640 versus 1280\,px.} Higher resolution helps YOLOv9m and \emph{hurts}

\Cref{tab:resolution} shows that ``higher resolution helps small objects'' does not hold as
a general claim on this data. It helps one architecture and hurts another, and AP-small
declines for both even where overall mAP50 rises. We flag this because the opposite is often
assumed. Notably, the best detector at 1280 (YOLOv9m) is \emph{not} the best counter
(\cref{sec:counting}) --- an early instance of the theme this paper develops.
