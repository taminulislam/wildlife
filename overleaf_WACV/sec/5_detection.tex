\section{Detection Benchmark}
\label{sec:detection}

We first establish that the detection stage is not the limiting factor. Twelve architectures
spanning two-stage, one-stage and query-based designs were trained on the identical
site-stratified split with identical preprocessing, which bounds how much of the counting
error any choice of backbone could account for.

\Cref{tab:detectors} reports every model on identical data. The first thing it shows is how
little separates them: AP$_{50}$ ranges from 0.365 to 0.510, with the top three within 0.004
of each other, across designs as different as Faster R-CNN, DINO and YOLO12m. That is the
signature of a data-limited rather than an architecture-limited regime, and it puts a ceiling
on how much of the counting error in \cref{sec:counting} any backbone choice could explain.

The absolute values are low, and two separate effects account for it. The first is
geometric. For two axis-aligned boxes of side $s$ whose centres differ by $d$ pixels,
$\mathrm{IoU}=(s-d)/(s+d)$; at the corpus median $s=27$ a four-pixel centre error already
drops IoU to 0.742 and fails a 0.75 threshold, and nine pixels fails 0.50. Scoring at the
upper IoU thresholds therefore demands sub-pixel localization on animals whose entire extent
is 27 pixels and whose thermal boundary is diffuse, and size-stratified metrics inherit the
same ceiling --- on the 71\% of the corpus below $32^2$, YOLO11m scores 0.081 against 0.291
on medium objects, a factor of 3.6.

The second effect is that these metrics count boxes rather than animals. Every frame of every
animal is scored separately, so an animal visible for 100 frames contributes 100 ground-truth
boxes, and one found in half of them scores 0.5 recall even though a single detection is all
that counting requires. The 11,476 background frames contribute nothing in either direction,
because precision, recall and F1 have no true-negative term, so a detector that correctly
fires on none of them earns no credit for it. Counted per animal instead, the same detectors
that score around 0.50 box recall find 220 of the 235 individually annotated deer in at least
one frame, and the full pipeline of \cref{sec:counting} reaches 82 of 83 on held-out video.
The distance between 0.50 and 0.94 is not a discrepancy but two metrics answering different
questions, and it is the first sign that detection accuracy is a poor proxy for counting
ability.

The models are also wrong in different ways, which matters more than the narrow spread in
their aggregate scores. YOLO12m and YOLOv9m operate at 0.929 and 0.917 precision against
roughly 0.45 recall, while RTMDet-m and Faster R-CNN R50 invert that entirely, reaching 0.779
and 0.723 recall at 0.33 and 0.39 precision. Because they sit on opposite sides of that
trade, a model's rank depends on which criterion is applied: YOLO11m is 1st under
IoU$\geq$0.50 and 4th under the counting criterion, while YOLOv8m moves the other way, from
5th to 1st. \Cref{fig:criterion} plots this as a
rank change: seven of the eleven models move at least two places, and YOLOv10m falls six, from
2nd under IoU$\geq$0.50 to 8th under the counting criterion, while ATSS R50 climbs from 8th
to 2nd. Selecting a detector for a
counting pipeline on its detection metric therefore does not select the model that counts
best, and which operating point is preferable is not a detection question at all: it depends
on whether the downstream stage can filter false candidates, which \cref{sec:counting}
measures directly.

One architectural pattern does survive the criterion change. The query-based detectors
underperform: DINO R50 reaches 0.365 AP$_{50}$ on a full native schedule, below plain Faster
R-CNN, and Deformable DETR did not converge within our compute budget. This is consistent
with the known difficulty query-based designs have with very small objects, and it holds for
both models of that type tested; RT-DETR-L, which targets that weakness specifically,
recovers to 0.434 but still trails the convolutional one-stage models.

Resolution behaves less predictably than the small-object literature would suggest.
\Cref{tab:resolution} shows that doubling the input to 1280\,px helps YOLOv9m
(+0.025 AP$_{50}$) and hurts YOLOv10m ($-0.040$); at 640 the two are tied at 0.498, and at
1280 they diverge by 0.065. AP-small declines for both even where overall AP$_{50}$ rises, so
the gap between small and medium objects is not primarily a resolution deficit. The best
detector at 1280 (YOLOv9m) is also not the best counter (\cref{sec:counting}), an early
instance of the divergence this paper characterizes.

Every counting experiment that follows uses YOLO11m, and the way it was selected illustrates
the section's argument on the production model itself. Under the strict criteria it is the
best model on the roster: highest F1 at IoU$\geq$0.50 (0.535) and at IoU$\geq$0.30 (0.623).
Relax the criterion toward counting and it slips --- 2nd under centre-in-box, 4th under
presence, where YOLOv8m leads at 0.660. A detector chosen by conventional detection
standards is therefore not the one that ranks best once the criterion matches the task.

We keep YOLO11m nonetheless, for a reason tied to what consumes its output: the confirmation
stage is starved of precision rather than recall, since it must reject tens of thousands of
false candidates per run (\cref{sec:counting}). Among the four models operating above 0.87
precision, YOLO11m has clearly the highest recall (0.503 against 0.450--0.453) and the best
F1 of that group (0.645 against 0.597--0.608).

The choice matters less than it might appear, and the reason is the section's own finding.
The eleven models span 0.145 in AP$_{50}$ and 0.20 in F1, but \cref{sec:counting} shows that
interventions changing the candidate pool far more than a backbone swap would --- including
one that raises the pool's reach from 74 to 82 of 83 animals --- move the final count by at
most three animals, and usually downward. A detector sitting within a few points of the
roster's best is not what limits this system.
