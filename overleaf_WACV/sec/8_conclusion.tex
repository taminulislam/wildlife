\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why the confirmation stage resists learning.}
The evidence points to supervision scarcity rather than model class. Pool C offers 207
positive tracks against 6,801 negatives; pool G offers 219 against 27,460. The positives are
also not cleanly separable: duplicate tracks --- fragments of an animal some other track
already covers --- sit \emph{between} the positives and the false candidates in every feature
we measured (median confidence 0.42, against 0.72 for primaries and 0.20 for false
candidates), so a confirmer receives contradictory supervision on the very examples that
distinguish counting from detection. This is consistent with \cref{tab:capacity}, where error
grows monotonically with capacity, and with the fact that growing the pool --- which
multiplies negatives without multiplying positives --- makes every method worse.

\paragraph{What would change the result.}
More annotated video, specifically more \emph{videos} rather than more frames, since the
positive count scales with animals and the negative count scales with frames. We estimate
from the capacity trend that a corpus with a few thousand positive tracks would put a
learned confirmer ahead, but we cannot verify that claim on 236 animals and do not assert
it.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The corpus is 236 animals from four sites in one region and one winter, and one site
contributes 56\% of the animals; per-site figures for the smallest site (15 animals) are
noisy. All results are single-species and single-season. Our counting metric is a per-video
aggregate; we report the stricter identity-matched figure (56.6\%) alongside it, and a
reader who prefers that definition should use it. Because no individual animal appears in
more than one video in this corpus, cross-session re-identification could not be evaluated
at all --- \cref{sec:reid} measures the within-video ceiling only. Finally, the
scale-augmentation remedy of \cref{sec:analysis} is diagnosed but not implemented; we report
the mechanism rather than a patched number.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to build a thermal wildlife counting system and found that the accuracy a
practitioner would try to buy with a better detector is being lost somewhere else. By
decomposing counting error into detection, association, and confirmation, we showed that on
held-out video our pipeline finds 98.8\% of animals, gives 88.0\% a distinct track, and
counts 62.7\% --- and that four independent improvements to the first two stages each made the
third worse.

The practical guidance is inverted from the field's default. For a counting application on
data of this scale: do not select a detector by its detection metric, because the counting
criterion re-ranks the models; do not evaluate on video the detector trained on, because
that inflates coverage by 33 points; do not assume a larger candidate pool is a better one;
and do not expect a learned confirmation stage to beat a hand-tuned rule until the corpus is
substantially larger than 236 individuals.

We release the dataset, per-individual annotations, evaluation harness, and the
decomposition tooling. The gap between 88\% reachable and 63\% counted is, we think, the
most useful thing we can hand to the next group working on this problem: it is large,
precisely localized, and entirely open.
