\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Corpus, annotation and splits}

The corpus is 32 nocturnal road-transect videos recorded with a vehicle-mounted FLIR
thermal camera across four sites in southern Illinois, eight videos per site. Frames are
$640\times512$ single-channel thermal at 60\,fps; the vehicle travels at 25--90\,km/h, so
the background translates continuously and the same animal is visible for a few seconds at
most. \Cref{fig:dataset} shows representative frames with their annotation, and
\cref{tab:dataset} summarizes the corpus.

The 236 animals are unevenly distributed across the four sites: SHB contributes 132
(56\% of the corpus), TON 51, SHW 38 and MAS 15. That imbalance is not incidental --- SHB
is the dense-group site where most of our counting error concentrates (\cref{sec:counting}),
and MAS is small enough that its per-site figures should be read as indicative only.

Every individual deer is annotated in CVAT as a distinct \emph{track}. This is the property
the paper depends on: one track is one animal, so the same annotation simultaneously serves
as per-frame detection ground truth, as association ground truth, as count ground truth
(the number of tracks in a video), and --- as \cref{sec:analysis} exploits --- as identity
labels for re-identification.

We split by \emph{video}, never by frame, and stratify by site so all four sites appear in
each split --- the deployment target is these same sites, and a frame-level split would leak
near-duplicate frames across the boundary. The result is 19 training videos (12,852 images),
4 validation (2,337) and 9 test (3,725), allocated by animal count to roughly
65/19/16\%.

Throughout the paper, \textbf{``unseen'' means the 13 validation and test videos the
detector never trained on}, containing 83 of the 236 deer. \Cref{sec:counting} shows why
this distinction is not pedantic: counting accuracy measured over all 32 videos, 19 of which
trained the detector, is 33 points higher than on unseen video alone.

\subsection{Two properties that shape every later result}

\paragraph{Object scale.}
The animals are small and the distribution is one-sided: median box $29\times24$ pixels
($\sqrt{\text{area}}=26.8$), 71\% below the COCO~\cite{lin2014coco} small-object threshold
of $32^2$, and \emph{zero} boxes in the COCO-large category. Resolution is therefore the
strongest single accuracy lever, which motivates the ablation in \cref{sec:detection}. It
also has a consequence we did not anticipate and report in \cref{sec:analysis}: the
training split contains no box above 96 pixels, and the detector fails on the handful of
animals that appear larger than that.

\paragraph{Annotation protocol.}
\label{sec:annotation}
Annotation follows the standard video workflow: a human places boxes on selected frames and
the tool interpolates between them. The resulting label noise is normally left
unquantified, and we measure it instead, because on a moving camera at 60\,fps an animal
travels a median 2.28 box-widths between the frames a human actually labelled --- far enough
that a linearly interpolated box can sit entirely off the animal.

The consequence is bidirectional: it injects label noise at training time \emph{and}
penalises correct detections at evaluation time. \Cref{sec:gtquality} quantifies the
evaluation half by scoring identical weights against human-placed frames only, and finds
that roughly a third of the apparent detection error is an annotation artefact rather than
a detector failure.
